The Paddle I Didn't Plan to Buy.

I went looking for a JOOLA. Two of them wouldn't clear the manufacturer's own verification check, for reasons I never worked out, and both went back before I ever swung one in a game. What I ended up with instead was a $195 paddle from Hawaii.

In this one: the demo that started it · the chip that wouldn't confirm · why a free cover changed my mind

Read

How this started

I went in to demo a paddle. I came out wanting one.

The Pickl'r in Rancho Santa Margarita will let you take a demo paddle out into open play, which is the only honest way to evaluate one. Reading specs tells you nothing. Hitting six balls against a wall tells you slightly less. You need an hour of real points, against people trying to beat you, before a paddle tells you the truth about itself.

The paddle I took out was the JOOLA Perseus Pro V. I wrote about that afternoon in No. 01 — I had gone in a skeptic, on the theory that a paddle is a paddle, and came out corrected. The 16mm core produced power I had not been producing on my own, and it produced it without me swinging harder. That is a specific and slightly addictive sensation. I decided I was buying a Perseus.

What I did not anticipate was that it would take two paddles and two returns to not end up with one.

The detour

Two paddles. Two failed check-ins. One app.

JOOLA builds a verification chip into the handle of its premium paddles. Mine had one. It never registered, and I never found out why.

I found the Perseus Pro IV on Amazon, marked down to $213, with a further promotional discount that brought it to $185 at checkout. The price was the entire appeal. This is a flagship — the shape Ben Johns actually plays — and I had been seeing it around $250. Here it was landing under two hundred. Amazon is an authorized JOOLA retailer. The listing looked like every other listing. I did not think twice.

Some background on why the next part matters. In 2024, JOOLA began embedding NFC chips in its paddles — the same near-field technology behind tap-to-pay. You download the JOOLA Infinity app, tap your phone against the handle, and the paddle's serial number populates on its own. You register it, you upload your receipt, and you get an extended warranty for the trouble. It is a nice piece of consumer engineering, and it exists for an unglamorous reason: high-end paddles are a two-hundred-dollar product with instantly recognizable branding and no moving parts, which is close to a perfect target for counterfeiters. Fakes flood the market. The chip is how the company fights back.

A friend of mine did think twice. When I told him what I had bought, he told me to be careful — that the counterfeit problem in this sport is worse than most people realize, and that the big marketplaces are where it surfaces.

The mechanism he described turns out to be a documented one, and it has two halves. Amazon pools identical products from different sellers into the same bin, keyed to the manufacturer's barcode — "commingled" inventory — so the unit that ships to you did not necessarily come from the seller you bought it from. Layered on top of that is a return-fraud pattern the trade calls product laundering: someone buys the real article, puts a convincing fake back in the box, and returns it. At the volume Amazon moves, not every return gets the kind of inspection that would catch a good fake. Amazon's position is that returns are inspected and graded before anything goes back into sellable stock. Sellers have spent years in Amazon's own forums saying plenty gets through regardless.

My paddle would not authenticate. I tapped it against the phone the way the instructions describe. I tried the other phone in the house. I re-read the steps, twice, on the assumption that the problem was me. Nothing.

Here is the part worth sitting with. Nothing else about the delivery suggested a problem. The box looked right. The printing looked right. There was extra grip tape and protective edge tape tucked in the sleeve, the way a premium paddle arrives. There were tags from Amazon and tags from JOOLA's warehouse in Germany. If you had handed me that package without an app in my pocket, I would have peeled it open, taped the handle, and gone and played with it happily for a year.

I sent it back. Amazon replaced it without argument. The replacement arrived in the same convincing packaging and failed the same check. That one went back too.

I want to be careful here, because I never learned what the actual problem was. Every sign I could see said those paddles were legitimate. The chip may have been faulty. The app may have been the issue. It may have been something on my end I never identified. I have no way of knowing, and I am not going to imply otherwise. I still think those two paddles were probably perfectly good. But "probably" is an odd thing to settle for when the manufacturer has built a verification system precisely because looking right stopped being proof of anything — and mine, twice, would not verify. A paddle you cannot register is also a paddle with no warranty standing behind it. So both went back. Amazon's returns were painless on both occasions, which is worth saying plainly.

None of which is a verdict on the paddle. I never got to swing either one of mine, and the Perseus is still a paddle I want — the demo at the Pickl'r saw to that. I would like to get a verified one in my hands, a Pro IV or a Pro V, and give it the long-term review it actually deserves. That piece is still coming.

By every sign I could see, it was the real thing. The chip in the handle just wouldn't say so.

Plan B

So I bought a paddle from Hawaii instead.

Two returns deep, and still swinging the paddle I had been trying to replace, I did what I should probably have done at the start: I stopped shopping for a brand and started reading the people who actually measure this equipment for a living.

One paddle kept surfacing. The Honolulu J6CR, from Honolulu Pickleball Co., a smaller outfit that has built a reputation among reviewers for doing genuinely odd, genuinely inventive things with paddle construction rather than following whatever the category leaders did last season. The independent testers were close to unanimous on it. One of the more skeptical reviewers in the space went in openly suspicious of the hype and came out impressed. Another, who tests full-foam paddles specifically, called it his favorite in that class.

It lists at $195. Which is to say: almost exactly what the discounted Perseus would have cost me, had the discounted Perseus been willing to admit what it was. I bought one.

Raw carbon, no paint. The hitting surface is left unfinished on purpose — that grey, chalky texture is the grit. Along the bottom edge: UPA Pro certified, designed and engineered in Hawaii.

What it's made of

Built like something that costs more.

The J6CR belongs to what the industry has started calling the fourth generation of paddle construction, and the headline departure is the core. Most paddles you have ever held are polypropylene honeycomb — a grid of plastic cells sandwiched between two faces. This one is foam, and not one uniform block of it but multiple densities, thermoformed into a perimeter-balanced shell.

The face is layered carbon fiber and fiberglass, with a raw carbon hitting surface on top. Raw means unpainted and unsealed, which is deliberate. That slightly rough, chalkboard-adjacent texture is what grabs the ball and makes it rotate. Independent lab testing has put the J6CR in the neighborhood of 2,300 revolutions per minute on spin, which is squarely competitive with paddles wearing much more expensive badges.

The trade-off with raw carbon, and it is worth knowing before you buy any paddle in this category, is that grit is a consumable. It wears down. It wears down faster if you drag the face on concrete. Honolulu has been teasing a longer-lasting surface treatment as a future option, which suggests they know it too.

  • Shape — elongated. 16.5″ long, 7.5″ wide, with a 6″ handle.
  • Core — 16mm, multi-density foam rather than honeycomb.
  • Face — layered carbon fiber and fiberglass; raw carbon hitting surface.
  • Weight — roughly 8.0 oz stock, which is light for an elongated paddle.
  • Swing weight — measured by reviewers in the 112–115 range.
  • Twist weight — around 6.2–6.4. Above average for its swing-weight class.

The spec I only recently learned to care about

Twist weight is a paddle's resistance to rotating in your hand when you hit the ball somewhere other than the middle. High twist weight means an off-center ball still goes roughly where you meant it to. Low twist weight means the paddle turns, and the ball sprays. It is, in other words, the forgiveness number — and it is the single spec I would most want a developing player to understand, because developing players miss the middle constantly.

What makes the J6CR interesting is the relationship between its two weight figures. Elongated paddles usually buy stability by being heavy through the head, which makes them slow at the net. This one is light and quick, and still posts a twist weight above what paddles in its swing-weight class typically manage. You are not paying for stability with hand speed, which is normally the deal.

The practical consequence is that it plays well straight out of the box. More on that in a moment, because I did try to improve on it, and I was wrong.

The value question

A top-tier paddle at a mid-tier price.

The premium end of this market now runs from about $220 to $280, and a good deal of what you are buying up there is a name and an endorsement contract. The J6CR sits under $200 and gives up nothing measurable to the tier above it. The spin numbers are competitive. The stability numbers are better than most of its weight class. The construction is more adventurous than most of what the large brands are shipping.

You are not buying a compromise. You are declining to fund a marketing budget. Those are different transactions, and only one of them costs you anything on the court.

The clip is the whole argument. Textured neoprene, bound edges, the J6CR sword-and-shield on the face and a UPA certification code in the corner — hung off the chain-link by its own strap, a step from the ball bin and inside arm's reach between games.

The thing in the box

And then there's the cover.

The J6CR ships with a waterproof paddle cover. Not a drawstring sleeve, not a branded dust bag — a properly made, weather-sealed cover, with a clip on it so you can hang the thing on the fence.

This sounds like a small detail and it is not. A paddle set down on a court is a paddle waiting to be stepped on, kicked, rained on, or dragged across the concrete face-first, which is the fastest way to sand the grit off a raw carbon face. The clip solves all of that for the cost of a bit of hardware.

For context: the JOOLA does not come with a cover at all. Neither do most paddles at any price. It is the sort of inclusion that costs the manufacturer a few dollars and tells you something about how the company thinks. The materials and the stitching here are better than the price of the paddle implies. It is the rare included accessory that does not immediately end up in a drawer.

Two strips of tape

One I took off. One I'd recommend to anybody.

Paddle tuning is a genuine rabbit hole, and the entry point is lead or tungsten tape added at the edges to change how the paddle swings. I added weighted tape to both sides of the J6CR, in the standard positions, and played with it for a stretch.

I took it off. I could not feel a difference — not a small difference I was talking myself out of, no difference at all. My honest read is that the paddle's stock twist weight was already doing the work I was trying to add, which is exactly what the reviewers said it would do. Weighting a paddle is a real technique and it helps real players. It did not help me, at my level, on this paddle.

The other strip stays on. Protective edge tape along the top of the paddle is a few dollars and about ninety seconds of work, and I recommend it without reservation. Mine is already visibly scuffed — which is the entire point. Every one of those marks is abrasion that would otherwise be on the carbon. When it looks bad enough to bother you, peel it off and put a fresh one on. It is the cheapest insurance in the sport.

The verdict

What I notice now that I didn't a year ago.

All in, I am very happy with this paddle. It does what I bought it to do, it forgives the mishits I still produce in volume, and at $195 it did not require me to pretend the purchase was an investment.

But the thing I keep turning over isn't really about the paddle. It is that I can tell the difference now. When I started, a paddle was a paddle — a flat thing you hit a ball with, and the only question was whether it felt heavy. Somewhere in the last year that changed. I notice weight. I notice where the balance sits. I notice grit, and when it is going. I notice twist weight, a spec I did not know existed and now think about every time I catch one off the tip and the ball still lands in.

That is what improvement actually feels like from the inside, and nobody tells you this part. It is not only that the shots get better. It is that you slowly acquire a vocabulary for what your hands have been feeling the whole time. A beginner cannot appreciate a well-designed paddle, because a beginner has nothing to compare it to. You earn the ability to notice.

And once you can notice, you start noticing what a company includes when nobody made them. Honolulu built a paddle that measures against equipment costing eighty dollars more, priced it under two hundred, and then put a beautiful waterproof cover in the box with a clip on it so it would hang on a fence. Not one person would have complained if they hadn't. That is the tell. ✦
