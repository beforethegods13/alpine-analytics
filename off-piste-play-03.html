The LT Pro 48:
I Normally Don’t Think About Balls.

For most of my first year I played with whatever came out of the bucket. Then someone opened a new tube before a game, and I spent the next hour trying to keep up with it. Our whole group plays nothing else now.

In this one: the ball that changed the game · who built it and why · how long any ball really lasts · a 100-ball group buy

On the cover: the tube makes its case — durable, sustainable, predictable. Three of those claims hold up on our courts. The fourth gets its own section inside.

How this started

I never gave the ball a second thought. Then I met this one.

When you are new to this game, you have more pressing concerns than the ball. Where to stand. When to come to the kitchen line. How to stop hitting the third shot into the net. The ball is simply the thing everyone is hitting, and whatever someone tossed out of the bag that night is what you play with. I never thought twice about it.

That lasted until one evening when a player cracked open a fresh tube of the Life Time LT Pro 48 before our first game. Within a few points I could tell something was different. It came off the paddle faster. It sat up higher off the bounce. It went, with unnerving consistency, precisely where it had been hit — which is wonderful when you hit it well and very revealing when you do not.

I will be honest: that first night, it was almost too much ball for me. Everything arrived a beat earlier than my hands expected. But a funny thing happened over the next few weeks. My timing caught up, and once it did, going back to the slower, softer, less predictable balls felt like playing in sand. The LT Pro 48 did not make me a better player. It made it much easier to tell whether I was becoming one.

Where it came from

A ball designed by a CEO who plays.

Most pickleballs are commodities. This one started as a personal project at the top of a public company.

The LT Pro 48 comes from Life Time, the Minnesota-based operator of upscale athletic clubs, and it was designed by the company's founder, chairman and CEO, Bahram Akradi, with his engineering team. Akradi is a trained engineer and a committed player, and by the company's own account he got tired of the same things the rest of us complain about: balls that bounce differently from one to the next, that fly unpredictably, and that crack after a handful of games. He set out to find out why, and to build something that didn't.

The ball debuted inside Life Time's own clubs in 2024, and the company filed a patent on the design in 2025. Then it went pro. In August 2025 it became the exclusive ball of the Carvana PPA Tour, making its tournament debut in Bristol, Tennessee, and in April 2026 Major League Pickleball named it the league's official ball starting with the 2026 season. The two organizations have operated under one umbrella, the United Pickleball Association, since 2024, which means the LT Pro 48 is now the ball in play at the top of the professional game.

One point of fairness, because the ball wars in this sport are real: it is not the only ball with an official title. Franklin's X-40 remains the official ball of USA Pickleball and the US Open. If you play sanctioned amateur tournaments, you will still see plenty of Franklins. But if you watch the pros on television, you are watching the LT Pro 48.

The company behind it

Life Time is not a ball company that happens to run a few gyms. It is the reverse, and the scale is considerable. Founded by Akradi in 1992 and traded on the New York Stock Exchange, it operates more than 190 resort-style athletic country clubs across the United States and Canada, with more than 800 permanent pickleball courts among them. It runs MIORA, a growing chain of performance and longevity clinics. It sells its own nutrition line. And it owns and produces more than thirty endurance events a year, including a name every mountain athlete knows: the Leadville Trail 100, the high-altitude mountain-bike and running races in the Colorado high country. A company that runs races at ten thousand feet has some standing to talk about durability.

What makes it different

Forty-eight holes, and why the number matters.

A standard outdoor pickleball has forty holes. The LT Pro 48 has, as advertised, forty-eight, and they are spaced symmetrically around the sphere. That sounds like a spec-sheet detail until you understand what it fixes. On a ball with uneven hole placement, the airflow differs depending on which side happens to be facing forward, so two identical swings can produce two slightly different flights. Even spacing means the ball behaves the same way no matter how it is oriented when you hit it. That is where the predictability comes from.

The second fix is at the edges of the holes themselves. On most balls, each hole is a sharp-edged cut, and sharp edges are where stress concentrates and where cracks begin. Life Time chamfers them — bevels the rim of each hole — which spreads the load of every impact more evenly and, per the company, reduces cracking while cleaning up the flight.

The rest of the recipe explains the speed. The ball is injection molded in two pieces, weighs 26 grams, and is rated to bounce about 33.5 inches when dropped from 78 inches. USA Pickleball's standards allow a bounce between 30 and 34 inches and a weight between roughly 22 and 26.5 grams. The LT Pro 48 sits near the top of both ranges: a heavier ball that holds its line in the wind, and a livelier one that rewards a firm swing. That is precisely the combination that felt like too much on my first night, and precisely the combination I now prefer.

How long it lasts

Every ball dies young. This one takes longer.

Here is something nobody tells you when you start: pickleballs are consumables, and not especially long-lived ones. Hard outdoor courts, fast hands and hot afternoons take their toll on every ball ever made. Eventually each one cracks, goes soft, or drifts just far enough out of round that it starts wobbling in flight. At the elite level, some pros say a ball is finished in a matter of hours.

The LT Pro 48 is not immune to any of that. Sooner or later, these will crack too. Thankfully, we haven't had one crack on us yet — but then, we are not pros. All things considered, it holds up to hard play better than anything else we have used. It keeps its bounce and its shape noticeably longer before it starts to go, and in a group that hits as hard as ours does, that difference adds up fast.

Which leads to a less comfortable question. If every ball has a short life, where do they all go when it ends?

The fourth claim on the tube

Half a billion balls a year, and nowhere for them to go.

By industry estimates, more than 500 million pickleballs are manufactured every year. Do the rough arithmetic at twenty-odd grams apiece and that is on the order of twelve thousand tons of hard plastic, most of it destined for a landfill within months of leaving the factory. Most curbside recycling programs will not take them, so a cracked ball tossed in the blue bin usually ends up in the trash anyway.

The good news is that the sport has started paying attention. BounceBack, a small startup, collects cracked balls through court-side bins and remolds them into new, pro-spec pickleballs — a genuinely closed loop. The nonprofit P3 Cares will send a free collection bin to any court that asks. Doorstep recycler Ridwell has run pickleball pilots with clubs in Portland, and Sarasota County, Florida, launched a court-side program this summer. Closer to home, a Santa Monica teen program called Another Bounce, working with the nonprofit Habits of Waste, just set a Guinness World Record by diverting more than 22,000 tennis balls and pickleballs from landfill.

So when a tube says "Sustainable," it is fair to ask what that means. Life Time's answer is durability: a ball that stays in play longer means fewer balls bought and fewer thrown away. That is a real argument, and on our courts the durability is real. But durability is the first step, not the whole answer. The companies making these balls could do considerably more, and none of it is exotic:

  • Take the balls back. A collection bin at every club and a prepaid mail-back option for everyone else. A company with more than 800 courts under its own roof is in an unusually good position to lead here.
  • Close the loop. Put recycled material into new balls, or partner with the people already doing it, and publish how much.
  • Say what it's made of. Mark the resin type clearly so recyclers know what they are handling.
  • Keep the packaging honest. The LT Pro 48 tube is paperboard rather than plastic, which is a good start. Bulk packs that skip individual tubes entirely are better still.

And there is a part for players. Retire a dead ball to the drill bucket before you throw it out. When it is truly finished, find a recycling bin rather than a trash can — or ask your court to request one.

How it compares

I've tried the others. I keep coming back.

One unexpected side effect of discovering that balls differ is that I now find it genuinely interesting to try new ones. Since that first night I have played with balls from Franklin, JOOLA, Vulcan and Selkirk, all of them respected names, all of them perfectly good balls.

The LT Pro 48 is still my favorite, and it is not especially close on the things I care about most. For bounce, it is the most consistent: the ball does the same thing off the court every time. For speed, it is the liveliest of the group, which rewards good contact and exposes bad contact in equal measure.

About that color

The most common complaint I hear about the LT Pro 48 has nothing to do with how it plays. It is the color. Players used to the classic neon yellow sometimes say it is harder to pick up. The explanation is simple: it is not yellow. Life Time makes it in a bright optic green, and next to a bucket of traditional balls, it does look different.

I will cast my vote plainly: I like it. I have had no trouble tracking it in daylight or under the lights at night, and I think it is a genuinely good-looking ball — closer to a piece of sports equipment than a highlighter. But color is personal, and eyes differ. If you have struggled to see it, I would not argue with you. I would just point out that it is the same color the pros are tracking at full speed on television.

The group vote

Ten players, one hundred balls, one decision.

I am not the only convert. The LT Pro 48 is now the ball of choice for our regular game at Rancho Mission Viejo — the RMV Mid-Week Pick'lers, ten of us at last count. Once enough of us had played with it, nobody wanted to go back, so we made it official: we bought a hundred of them and split the cost.

A practical note for any group thinking of doing the same. Buying in bulk changes the math considerably. In our experience, a large pack came close to halving the per-ball price compared with buying individual tubes. Split ten ways, that is a very reasonable price for a season's supply of the same ball the pros use. It also settles, permanently, the pre-game question of whose balls we are playing with.

The verdict

The thing you notice once you start noticing.

The LT Pro 48 is fast, true and tough. It asks more of you in the first few games than a slower ball does, and it gives back more once you meet it there. It lasts longer than anything else we have tried. And for a rec player, there is a small, undeniable pleasure in knowing you are hitting the same ball the best players in the world are hitting that weekend.

Last time in this column I wrote about learning to notice paddles — weight, balance, grit, twist. It turns out the ball was the next lesson, and I had been holding it the whole time. A year ago I would have told you a pickleball is a pickleball. I no longer believe that, and our group has a hundred reasons sitting in a bucket to prove it.

I would still like to see Life Time put a real recycling program behind the word on the tube. A ball this good deserves an ending as well engineered as the rest of it. ✦
